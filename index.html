Offline-to-Online RL and Goal-Conditioned Curriculum Learning
for Long-Horizon Kitchen Manipulation

Andrew Li, Xia Chu, Zixin Guo
Robotics Institute, Carnegie Mellon University
16-831 Introduction to Robot Learning — Spring 2026 Course Project

Abstract

We address sparse-reward, long-horizon manipulation in the 9-DoF Franka Kitchen environment, where an agent must complete four sequential subtasks (microwave, kettle, bottom burner, light switch). Standard online RL struggles because meaningful reward signals are extremely rare under random exploration. We benchmark a random baseline, PPO (on-policy), SAC (off-policy), and MBPO (model-based), then study two modifications: (M1) Offline-to-Online RL with IQL pretraining followed by SAC fine-tuning, and (M2) PPO with goal-conditioned curriculum learning and dense reward shaping. M1 lifts mean return well above all online baselines within 800k env steps, while M2 is the only method to consistently solve all four tasks (given a longer 20M-step budget).

Modification 1: Offline-to-Online RL (IQL→SAC)

Sparse rewards make pure online RL struggle to discover any reward signal early in training. We exploit the D4RL/kitchen/complete-v2 expert dataset \(\mathcal{D} = \{(s, a, r, s')\}\) and split learning into two stages:

Offline phase. We pretrain a policy with Implicit Q-Learning (IQL): a conservative value function \(Q_\theta(s,a)\) is learned without evaluating out-of-distribution actions, and the policy is updated by advantage-weighted regression toward dataset actions: $$\max_{\pi_\phi}\;\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[ \exp\!\bigl(\beta\cdot A_\theta(s,a)\bigr)\,\log\pi_\phi(a\mid s)\right].$$ This produces a policy that already executes meaningful behaviors and achieves non-zero return without exploring from scratch.

Online phase. We then fine-tune with SAC, mixing newly collected on-policy transitions with the offline buffer (RLPD-style). The Q-function is recalibrated for a few thousand steps before policy updates resume, preventing the conservative offline value from collapsing once it sees fresh data.

Result. IQL→SAC reaches mean return ≈2.5 by 250k online steps—far above any from-scratch baseline—and reliably solves both microwave and kettle. See the red curve in the comparison figure above.

Modification 2: Goal-Conditioned Curriculum Learning

Long-horizon credit assignment is the bottleneck for from-scratch policies: the agent must execute several qualitatively different subtasks within one episode. We address this with a two-phase curriculum and a goal-conditioned PPO policy.

Goal-conditioned policy. We append a 4-dim one-hot current task indicator \(\mathbf{g}_t \in \{0,1\}^4\) to the observation, so the policy \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s_t, \mathbf{g}_t)\) can specialize per subtask. \(\mathbf{g}_t\) is selected by a deterministic rule: scan tasks in a fixed easy→hard order and pick the first whose Euclidean distance to its goal exceeds \(\delta=0.3\). Once a task succeeds, \(\mathbf{g}_t\) auto-advances to the next pending task.

Dense reward shaping. On top of the sparse +1-per-task reward, we add the per-step reduction in goal-distance, $$ r_t = r_t^{\text{sparse}} + \alpha \cdot \sum_k\bigl(d^{(k)}_{t-1} - d^{(k)}_t\bigr),\;\alpha = 1.0. $$ This gives the policy a continuous learning signal even when no task has been completed.

Phase 1 — Isolated single-task training.  16M steps. Each episode resets to a single uniformly sampled subtask; \(\mathbf{g}_t\) is fixed for the episode and reward only considers the active task. Episodes terminate immediately on success. This avoids the difficulty of multi-task credit assignment.

Phase 2 — Sequential multi-task fine-tuning.  4M steps. \(\mathbf{g}_t\) is re-evaluated every step on the full 4-task sequence, and the dense reward is summed over all tasks.

Analysis

Learning curves

Among the from-scratch online baselines, SAC reaches non-trivial returns (peak 1.73 ± 0.53) earliest thanks to its replay buffer, but oscillates heavily after 250k steps. PPO learns more slowly but is steadier—it converges around mean return 0.5–0.75 by 600k steps. MBPO briefly discovers reward signal around 60k steps but its ensemble model amplifies errors under 95% synthetic data, and the policy collapses back to zero. Random remains at ≈0.

Task-relevant metrics

Per-task completion plots reveal that PPO and SAC reliably solve different tasks: PPO learns kettle (53% across seeds), while SAC learns the bottom burner and light switch on the seed that converges. Neither baseline solves microwave. IQL→SAC is the only method to reach a non-trivial microwave completion rate within 800k steps. PPO Curriculum is the only method that consistently solves all four when given its full 20M budget.

Failure cases

Three distinct failures are observable. (i) SAC seed sensitivity: only 1 of 3 SAC seeds escapes the zero-return basin; the other two never see reward. (ii) PPO microwave: 0/3 PPO seeds learn microwave under sparse reward—exploring the precise grasp→door-open contact sequence is too unlikely for on-policy rollouts. The single-task diagnostic shows PPO needs ~3M steps and dense shaping to crack microwave even in isolation. (iii) MBPO collapse: the SAC policy inside MBPO overfits to model errors under the standard 5% real / 95% synthetic mixing ratio. Training reward decays from 0.087 (160k steps) down to 0.028 (800k steps), exactly the failure mode reported by the original MBPO paper for sparse-reward manipulation.

Comparison across all methods

Ranked by final mean return at 800k steps: IQL→SAC (1.57) > PPO Curriculum (1.20) > SAC (0.63) > PPO (0.53) > MBPO (0.00) = Random (0.00). The two modifications dominate every from-scratch baseline by a wide margin, confirming that either a strong prior (offline data) or structured exploration (curriculum + dense reward) is necessary to make progress on this 4-task sparse-reward problem.

Comparison across modifications

IQL→SAC vs. PPO Curriculum trade off in interesting ways. IQL→SAC is dramatically more sample efficient on the sub-tasks present in its offline data (microwave, kettle): it reaches mean return >2 within 250k online steps and inherits "bootstrap" behaviors directly from the demonstrations. However, it inherits the dataset's coverage bias—bottom burner and light switch, which are under-represented in complete-v2, are not solved. PPO Curriculum is much slower in wall-clock and gradient steps (20M vs 800k), but is self-contained: it only needs the environment plus a one-line distance heuristic for goal cycling and reaches a 100% completion rate across all four tasks. In short: M1 is the right choice when expert data is available; M2 is the right choice when it isn't.

Conclusion & Future Work

Conclusion

On long-horizon, sparse-reward Franka Kitchen, all four from-scratch baselines (random, PPO, SAC, MBPO) plateau well below the achievable return of 4.0 within 800k env steps, with the best seed (SAC) reaching only 1.9. Both proposed modifications cleanly beat the baselines: IQL→SAC offline-to-online achieves the highest 800k-step performance (1.57 mean return), and PPO with goal-conditioned curriculum + dense rewards is the only method to consistently complete all four sequential subtasks (when given a 20M-step budget). The two modifications target different bottlenecks—reward sparsity (M1, via expert data) and credit assignment (M2, via curriculum and shaping)—and either is sufficient to make this benchmark tractable.

Future work

  • Combine M1 and M2: use IQL pretraining for the early tasks (where data exists) and goal-conditioned curriculum for fine-tuning to cover the remaining tasks. This should match PPO Curriculum's coverage at a fraction of the gradient steps.
  • Learned high-level policy: replace the rule-based easy→hard task selector with a small classifier trained on expert trajectories, removing the manual ordering hyperparameter.
  • Robust MBPO: the current MBPO failure suggests uncertainty-aware rollouts (e.g., terminating synthetic trajectories when ensemble disagreement spikes) may be needed for sparse-reward manipulation tasks.
  • Real-robot transfer: all current results are in simulation. Transferring the IQL-pretrained policy to a real Franka arm would test how much of the offline prior survives the sim-to-real gap.
